Must know first

Skin = epidermis + dermis. Hypodermis lies deep to skin.

Epidermis: keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, ectodermal.
Dermis: connective tissue, mesodermal.
Thick skin: palms/soles, no hair, prominent stratum corneum + lucidum.

01 • Overview

What the integumentary system does

Barrier + protection

Mechanical, permeability, biologic, and ultraviolet protection.

Homeostasis

Regulates body temperature and water loss; sweat participates in excretion.

Sensation + signaling

Conveys sensory information, contributes to immune surveillance, and participates in endocrine functions including vitamin D3 activation.

High yield: The terms thick skin and thin skin refer to epidermal thickness, not total skin thickness. Thick skin is found on palms and soles, is hairless, and has a very thick stratum corneum. Elsewhere is thin skin, usually with hair follicles.

02 • Epidermis

Know the strata from deep → superficial

LayerKey histologyMain eventExam clue
Stratum basaleSingle layer; small cuboidal to low columnar cells on basal laminaMitotically active stem/progenitor cells; new keratinocytesDesmosomes laterally; hemidesmosomes to basal lamina; melanin may be present
Stratum spinosumSeveral layers; “prickle” appearanceKeratin synthesis increases; tonofilaments bundle into tonofibrilsSpines/intercellular bridges are desmosomal attachments; nodes of Bizzozero
Stratum granulosum1–3 layers of flattened cells with intensely basophilic granulesKeratohyalin granules + lamellar bodies; terminal differentiationFilaggrin/trichohyalin aggregate keratin; lipid secretion helps water barrier
Stratum lucidumPale/refractile layerAdvanced keratinization; nuclei/organelles disappearingCharacteristic of thick skin
Stratum corneumFlattened, desiccated, anucleate squamesKeratin-filled cells + lipid barrier; desquamationThickest variable layer; extreme in palms/soles and calluses
Mnemonic, deep → superficial: Basale → Spinosum → Granulosum → Lucidum → Corneum. In thin skin, skip lucidum.

Keratinization sequence

1. Basal

Free ribosomes synthesize keratins → intermediate filaments (tonofilaments).

2. Spinous

Keratin filaments increase and form visible bundles called tonofibrils.

3. Granular

Keratohyalin granules appear; filaggrin/trichohyalin promote aggregation. Lamellar bodies release lipids.

4. Cornified

Nucleus and organelles are lost; plasma membrane thickens; mature cells desquamate.

Numbers worth memorizing: Keratinization from granulosum to corneum takes roughly 2–6 hours. Total epidermal turnover is approximately 47 days: ~1–2 days for basal mitoses, ~31 days through spinous/granular compartments, and ~14 days through the corneum. In psoriasis, turnover can shorten to about 8–10 days.

Epidermal water barrier

The barrier depends on terminally differentiating keratinocytes and has two major structural components: an insoluble cell envelope on the inner plasma-membrane surface and an external lipid envelope. Lamellar bodies discharge lipid-rich contents between the stratum granulosum and stratum corneum. Severe loss of this barrier, as in extensive burns, can cause dangerous fluid loss.

Melanocyte pathway

Melanin synthesis occurs by oxidation of tyrosine → DOPA by tyrosinase, followed by conversion to melanin in premelanosomes/melanosomes. Melanosomes move into dendritic processes and are transferred to keratinocytes by pigment donation (cytocrine secretion). The pigment forms supranuclear caps that help protect nuclear DNA from UV injury.

Epidermal–melanin unit: approximately 1 melanocyte : 36 keratinocytes. The chapter emphasizes that melanocyte-to-keratinocyte ratios are broadly similar across races; differences in pigmentation involve melanosome production, distribution, and degradation.

04 • Dermis

Papillary vs reticular dermis

FeaturePapillary layerReticular layer
PositionSuperficial, directly beneath epidermisDeep
CT typeLoose connective tissueDense irregular connective tissue
CollagenThin fibers; mainly types I and IIIThick irregular bundles; mostly type I
CellularityMore cellularLess cellular
Vessels / nervesCapillaries and sensory endings, especially in dermal papillaeLarger vessels, appendages, deeper receptors
Epidermal–dermal junction: dermal papillae project upward while epidermal/rete ridges project downward. The increased interface strengthens attachment and is especially prominent where mechanical stress is high. In thick palmar/plantar skin, dermal ridges contribute to dermatoglyphics (fingerprints/footprints).

Langer’s lines reflect organized lines of tension formed by collagen and elastic fibers in the reticular dermis. Incisions parallel to these lines generally heal with less scarring.

05 • Sensory nerve receptors

Location often gives away the answer

Free nerve endings

Most numerous. Fine touch, temperature, and pain; may extend into basal epidermis and surround hair follicles.

Meissner corpuscle

Light touch. Small, elongated, in dermal papillae just beneath epidermis, especially hairless skin of fingers/toes and lips.

Pacinian corpuscle

Deep pressure and vibration. Large “onion-like” concentric lamellae in deeper dermis/hypodermis.

Ruffini corpuscle

Responds to skin stretch and torque; elongated encapsulated receptor associated with collagen displacement.

Microscope shortcut: Meissner = superficial papilla. Pacinian = deep onion.

06 • Epidermal appendages

Hair, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands

Hair follicle

Hair follicles are epidermal downgrowths present over almost the entire body. The expanded inferior end is the hair bulb, which contains proliferating matrix cells around a connective tissue dermal papilla. Matrix differentiation forms the hair shaft.

StructureHigh-yield fact
Hair shaftMedulla, cortex, cuticle. Cortex forms about 80% of hair mass.
Internal root sheathHenle layer + Huxley layer + internal root sheath cuticle.
External root sheathContinuous with epidermis.
Follicular bulgeStem-cell reservoir near insertion of arrector pili muscle.
Arrector piliSmooth muscle; contraction erects hair (“goose flesh”) and can assist sebum discharge.

Sebaceous glands

Develop from the external root sheath of hair follicles. They produce lipid-rich sebum by holocrine secretion: the entire secretory cell degenerates and becomes part of the secretion. Sebum enters the follicular infundibulum through the pilosebaceous canal.

Histology: Look for pale/foamy lobules attached to a hair follicle. Peripheral basal cells are smaller/darker; central cells become lipid-filled and break down.

07 • Nails

Hard keratin plates

Nails are plates of keratinized cells containing hard keratin. The proximal nail root/matrix contains proliferating keratinocytes that differentiate and contribute to the growing nail plate. The nail plate moves distally over the nail bed, while surrounding skin forms the nail folds.

Nail matrix/root

Main growth zone; proliferating keratinocytes produce hard keratin.

Nail plate

Dense keratinized plate.

Nail bed

Epithelium and underlying dermis beneath the plate.

08 • Clinical correlations

What tends to become exam questions

Basal cell carcinoma

Most common epidermal-origin skin cancer in the chapter. Resembles basal cells; typically slow-growing and usually does not metastasize.

Squamous cell carcinoma

Atypical keratinocytes can involve all epidermal levels; basement membrane disruption permits invasion/spread.

Malignant melanoma

Originates from melanocytes. Early radial growth may remain relatively superficial; vertical growth is associated with greater metastatic potential. ABCD: asymmetry, border irregularity, color variation, diameter >6 mm.

Psoriasis

Hyperproliferative epidermis with shortened turnover (~8–10 days in the chapter), producing thickened, scaly plaques.

Skin repair

Primary union occurs in clean, approximated wounds such as sutured surgical incisions and generally produces less scar. Secondary union occurs when wound edges are separated and tissue loss is greater, requiring more granulation tissue and contraction.

Dermal repair includes clot formation, removal of damaged collagen, granulation tissue formation, reepithelialization, fibroblast proliferation and migration, myofibroblast differentiation/wound contraction, and extracellular matrix deposition/remodeling. Basal keratinocytes near the wound proliferate and migrate beneath the scab. The chapter notes that migration can begin within hours and that adnexal epithelial stem-cell reservoirs—especially hair follicles—help reepithelialize wounds when epidermis is lost.

Burn clue: Massive destruction of all epithelial structures, as in a full-thickness/third-degree burn, removes local epithelial sources for reepithelialization; grafting may be required.
